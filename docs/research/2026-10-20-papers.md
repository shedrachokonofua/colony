# Autonomous software engineering: what the published record says (2023–2026)

Scope: papers, technical reports and first-party benchmark documentation read directly on 2026-10-20. Numbers are as reported by the authors unless marked "derived"; benchmark scores are not comparable across scaffolds, model generations or benchmark versions. Colony-internal figures are operator-reported and marked as such. 2026 papers are preprints unless stated otherwise; I did not independently replicate any result.

## What this means for Colony

1. **Simplicity is a real baseline, not a straw man.** A fixed localise → repair → validate pipeline (Agentless) reached 32.00% on SWE-bench Lite for $0.70, the best open-source score at the time, with no free-roaming agent loop ([Agentless, 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.01489)); in 2026, five of six multi-agent workflows trailed a single-agent reference by 1.71–8.76 points ([Do More Agents Help?, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.05670)), and a 260-configuration controlled study found multi-agent relative performance ranged from +80.8% to −70.0% depending on task structure, with sequential planning the worst case ([Scaling Agent Systems, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.08296)).
2. **Role-play multi-agent pipelines are the weakest form of decomposition.** On the same benchmark and base model, MetaGPT's SOP/document design beat ChatDev's dialogue-only roles on executability (3.75 vs 2.25) and human revision cost (0.83 vs 2.5) ([MetaGPT, 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.00352)); MAST later analysed 1,642 traces across seven frameworks and found pervasive specification, coordination and verification failures ([MAST, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13657)).
3. **Put the frontier model where orchestration and judgement are.** A small-model multi-agent system with a strong orchestrator beat larger single models, and the gain was attributed to "orchestrator capacity rather than sub-agent capacity" ([Can Small Agents Collaborate?, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.11327)); Colony's frontier architect/reviewer tier is better justified than making every implementer frontier-tier.
4. **Task length, not wall-clock, is the binding constraint.** METR's 50% time horizon was ~50 minutes for Claude 3.7 Sonnet and ~110 minutes for o3, with 80% horizons roughly 5× shorter ([METR, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14499)); a 3-hour run is therefore a multi-attempt or checkpointed task even for a frontier model, and the paper offers no evidence that a flash-tier model can sustain a 3-hour task in one run.
5. **Serial chains compound failure, and longer runs do not fix stuck work.** HORIZON finds non-linear collapse and a shift toward planning/memory failures as horizons grow, with process-level failures in 72.5% of failed traces ([HORIZON, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.11978)); CodeMonkeys found that raising the iteration cap does not rescue stuck serial state machines, whereas fresh parallel attempts keep scaling ([CodeMonkeys, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.14723)).
6. **Verification is the best-evidenced reliability lever, but it can be gamed.** Test-based selection lifted CodeMonkeys from 45.8% (random selection) to 57.4%, and a cross-submission ensemble to 66.2% ([CodeMonkeys](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.14723)); MAST attributes 8.2% of failures to no/incomplete verification and 9.1% to incorrect verification ([MAST](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13657)); but SpecBench found every frontier agent saturated the visible test suite while the held-out gap grew 28 percentage points per tenfold code-size increase ([SpecBench, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.21384)). A reviewer that reads the diff is not redundant with a suite the implementer can optimize against.
7. **Do not let the implementer grade its own work.** Self-validation rates fell from 100% on Sonnet to 0% on Gemini Flash across the capability spectrum ([Architecture as Capability Equalizer, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21747)); a strong-tier, independent reviewer is a structural requirement, not an expensive extra.
8. **Spend human time on the contract and the final judgement, not on per-turn supervision.** Human-written requirements raised GPT-5 from 8.40% to 25.9% on SWE-bench Pro ([SWE-bench Pro, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.16941)); an RCT of 16 experienced developers on mature repositories found that AI assistance increased completion time by 19% ([METR RCT, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089)).
9. **Harness tuning is worth double digits when context is tight.** With unchanged weights and tasks, mechanically shortening old tool results and reacting to stalls raised per-task fail-to-pass fraction from 28% to 49% and complete solutions from 43 to 72 on 169 tasks with a 20,480-token window ([Same Model, Different Harness, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26218)); planning is an accuracy scaffold for weaker models but mainly a cost saver for stronger ones ([Harness Design, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20804)).
10. **Environment parity is part of the verifier.** OpenAI built a containerised Docker harness because SWE-bench environments were hard to set up reliably and could fail valid solutions ([SWE-bench Verified, 2024](https://openai.com/index/introducing-swe-bench-verified/)); SWE-bench Pro, Terminal-Bench 2.0 and Commit0 all make the task environment part of the benchmark ([SWE-bench Pro](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.16941), [Terminal-Bench 2.0](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.11868), [Commit0](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.01769)). Colony's sandbox/CI mismatch is an observed failure class, not an edge case.

## 1. Multi-agent role pipelines vs one strong agent with tools

### 1.1 Four different comparisons are usually conflated

The literature varies at least four things that are often reported as "agents vs multi-agent":

1. **Control structure**: a free-roaming agent loop versus a fixed pipeline (SWE-agent vs Agentless).
2. **Action space and interface**: shell/JSON actions versus executable code versus a purpose-built agent-computer interface (CodeAct, SWE-agent).
3. **Role decomposition**: one controller versus several roles with handoffs (MetaGPT, ChatDev).
4. **Test-time compute allocation**: one attempt versus many samples, searches or debate rounds (CodeMonkeys, SWE-Search, LLM-Debate).

The answers differ because the comparisons hold different variables fixed. The cleanest evidence is summarised in Table 1.

Three readings of Table 1 matter for Colony.

First, **Agentless is the strongest simplicity result**: a fixed pipeline with LLM calls but no free-roaming controller beat many open-source agent scaffolds on SWE-bench Lite at a fraction of the cost. The lesson is not "agents are useless"; it is that the agent loop has to earn its cost. A factory that already knows the shape of the work (localise, change, test, review) should prefer the fixed shape and spend model tokens on the hard sub-steps.

Second, **SWE-agent, CodeAct and AutoCodeRover are interface results, not orchestration results**. The same model behaves differently when the toolset, feedback and action representation change. That is directly relevant to Colony's reviewer/implementer disagreements: if the implementer sees a shell and the reviewer sees a diff, they are not evaluating the same object.

Third, **MetaGPT vs ChatDev is the cleanest role-pipeline comparison available**, because both systems target the same benchmark, use the same base model in the comparison, and report the same metrics. MetaGPT's structured documents and SOPs beat ChatDev's dialogue-only roles on nearly every metric: executability 3.75 vs 2.25, runtime 541 s vs 762 s, productivity 124.3 vs 248.9 tokens per line, human revision cost 0.83 vs 2.5. ChatDev used fewer tokens overall (19,292 vs 31,255) and fewer tokens per line only because it produced less code (1.9 vs 5.1 files). Caveats: this is a 2023 benchmark with 2023 models, self-reported by MetaGPT's authors, and MAST later found both systems have high failure rates under independent analysis.

### 1.2 MAST: the independent check on role pipelines

MAST ("Why Do Multi-Agent LLM Systems Fail?") is the most useful corrective to vendor-reported multi-agent gains. It introduces a taxonomy built from 150+ annotated traces, then annotates 1,642 traces across seven popular multi-agent frameworks in coding, maths and general-agent domains ([MAST, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13657)). The headline: multi-agent performance gains on popular benchmarks "are often minimal", and the failures cluster into three categories and 14 modes. Section 2 gives the full taxonomy and rates.

Two MAST findings deserve emphasis here:

- **Verification is often theatre.** The authors write that "many existing verifiers perform only superficial checks, despite being prompted to perform thorough verification, such as checking if the code compiles or if there are leftover TODO comments" ([MAST](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13657)).
- **More roles do not automatically mean more checking.** In MAST's AG2 (MathChat) intervention, a better prompt with an explicit verification section significantly outperformed the baseline, while a new three-role topology did not: the authors report a Wilcoxon p-value of 0.4, so the small gain was not statistically significant ([MAST, Appendix H](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13657)).

That second result is the most directly transferable to Colony: adding a role (or a review epoch, or a steering agent) is not the same as adding verification. What helped was a clearer contract and a verification step, not a new org chart.

### 1.3 The 2026 equal-budget comparisons

The 2026 literature is much more careful about holding compute constant, and the results are less favourable to multi-agent than the 2023 role-play papers.

**Do More Agents Help?** compares six multi-agent workflow configurations against a single-agent reference ("BenchAgent Core") across ten reasoning, coding and tool-use benchmarks, all on GPT-4.1, with shared tool and scoring interfaces and shared step limits. It reports 3,302 instances per configuration per run and three runs. EvoAgent reaches 79.70% mean pooled accuracy versus 76.65% for Core and beats Core in all three runs; the other five workflows trail Core by 1.71–8.76 percentage points. LLM-Debate gains 9.67 points on HumanEval but loses 1.96 points in pooled accuracy while consuming 1.39× Core's tokens; ChatEval gains 22.25 points on IFEval but trails Core in pooled accuracy at 4.34× the tokens ([Do More Agents Help?, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.05670)).

**Single-agent vs multi-agent under equal thinking-token budgets.** Tran and Kiela make an information-theoretic argument that, under a fixed reasoning-token budget and perfect context utilisation, a single agent is more information-efficient. Empirically, across three model families on multi-hop reasoning, single-agent systems "consistently match or outperform" multi-agent systems when reasoning tokens are held constant. Much of the apparent multi-agent advantage is better explained by "unaccounted computation and context effects rather than inherent architectural benefits"; they also flag inadequate budget control (notably in Gemini 2.5) and benchmark-specific artefacts ([Single-agent LLMs Outperform MAS, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02460)). Caveat: this is multi-hop reasoning, not software engineering.

**Architecture-task alignment, not agent count.** A 260-configuration controlled study across six benchmarks, five architectures (single-agent plus Independent, Centralized, Decentralized and Hybrid multi-agent variants) and three model families found that relative performance versus a single agent ranged from +80.8% on decomposable financial reasoning to -70.0% on sequential planning. Coordination yields diminishing returns once the single-agent baseline is strong; tool-heavy tasks incur multi-agent overhead; and architectures without centralized verification propagate errors more than those with centralized coordination. The authors predict the best architecture for 87% of held-out configurations and find consistent preferences on unseen frontier models ([Scaling Agent Systems, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.08296)). The implication for Colony is not "never decompose"; it is that a serial dependency chain is the worst case for multi-agent coordination, while decomposable, independently checkable work is the best case.

**Small agents plus a strong orchestrator.** A minimal multi-agent design (one orchestrator plus small specialists, with restricted communication) can outperform substantially larger single-agent models, even when the single agent has direct tool access. The gain is driven by "orchestrator capacity rather than sub-agent capacity"; enabling reasoning in sub-agents gives limited or negative benefit ([Can Small Agents Collaborate?, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.11327)). This is the strongest published argument for Colony's tiering: the frontier model belongs at the architect/orchestrator, not necessarily at every worker.

**Minimal harness vs elaborate harness under equal time.** In an ML-engineering setting, under an equal time budget and the same frontier backbone, open-source state-of-the-art harnesses showed "no advantages" over a single session of a minimal-harness coding agent with read/write/bash primitives. The authors conclude that the backbone is the primary driver and that "the machinery layers become redundant in the coding agent setting", with poor returns from elaborating hand-crafted harnesses for current MLE benchmarks ([How Much of a Harness Does a Strong Agent Need?, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.40303)). Caveat: MLE benchmarks, not repository maintenance; the result is about _current MLE benchmarks_, and the authors say so.

**Harness changes matter with unchanged weights.** A controlled comparison of two configurations of the same harness — control: full conversation in time order; treatment: mechanically shorten older tool results as context fills, and react to repeated or stalled work — found that the treatment raised mean per-task fail-to-pass fraction from 28% to 49% and complete solutions from 43 to 72, on 169 SWE-bench Verified tasks with a 20,480-token window and a fixed 480-second attempt endpoint. The frozen treatment also improved both endpoints for three additional models without model-specific retuning; on the wide-window Qwen3.6 comparisons the arms were close on Verified and Pro, while FeatureBench retained a higher F2PF under treatment. The authors' conclusion is exactly the right frame: "coding-agent evaluations should treat the model and harness together as the tested solver" ([Same Model, Different Harness, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26218)).

**Component-level harness ablations.** A 176-setting study across four models, SWE-bench Verified and Terminal-Bench 2.1, five context-management strategies and four context-window budgets finds that context management becomes more valuable as the budget tightens, with most of the benefit coming from preventing context-overflow failures; staging rule-based elision before LLM summarisation is the strongest overall strategy, while making elided content recoverable adds machinery the models rarely use and yields no accuracy gain. Planning "shifts from an accuracy scaffold for weaker models to a cost saver for stronger models, with little change in accuracy". Predefined tools help models with weaker bash proficiency, while bash-capable models can use a bash-only interface at substantially lower cost, especially on command-line tasks ([An Empirical Study of Harness Design for Coding Agents, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20804)).

### 1.4 What survives

The evidence does **not** support a blanket "multi-agent is better" or "single agent is better" claim. It supports a narrower set of design rules:

- **Decompose only where the work is genuinely separable and the handoff is machine-checkable.** MetaGPT's document-based handoffs work better than ChatDev's dialogue because the artifact is inspectable; Agentless's fixed pipeline works because each phase has a clear interface. Colony's plan → implement → review → merge handoffs are artifact-based in principle; the failure mode to watch is when the handoff becomes a conversation (review comments, steering messages) rather than a diff, test result or decision record.
- **Put model strength at the controller and the verifier.** The small-agents result and the self-validation collapse both point the same way: the orchestrator and the reviewer need capability; the worker needs a bounded task and a good harness.
- **Do not add roles to fix reliability.** MAST's topology intervention (p = 0.4) and the 2026 equal-budget comparisons are consistent: extra roles add coordination failure surface and tokens. Add verification, not roles.
- **Treat the harness as part of the model.** "Same Model, Different Harness" and the harness-design study show double-digit effects from context and tool changes. Colony's tier-specific harness (flash vs strong vs frontier) should be tuned per tier, not shared as an unexamined default.
- **Beware the equal-time result.** The minimal-harness MLE study says the backbone dominates under equal time; the harness study says harness details matter, especially under tight context. Both can be true: if the task is within the backbone's horizon and the harness is not pathological, the model dominates; if the harness wastes context or the task is beyond the horizon, the harness and the decomposition dominate. Colony's failure profile is the second regime.

## 2. Failure taxonomies, and Colony's five failures

Colony's reliability stack — fault classification, attempt budgets, review epochs, stall caps, liveness watchdogs, autosave, steering, scope parking — is a set of scaffolding components layered on the same agent loop. The literature on failure taxonomies and component interaction is therefore directly applicable, and it is less comforting than a per-mechanism reading.

### 2.1 MAST: a taxonomy for multi-agent systems

MAST is built from 150+ traces with expert annotators, then scaled to 1,642 annotated traces across seven frameworks; human inter-annotator agreement is κ = 0.88, and the released LLM-as-a-judge pipeline reaches F1 = 0.80 with few-shot prompting ([MAST, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13657)). The 14 modes and their reported occurrence rates are in Table 2.

| Category                      | Failure modes (reported rate)                                                                                                                                                                                           | What it looks like in practice                                                                                              |
| ----------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| FC1: System design            | 1.1 Disobey task specification (11.8%); 1.2 Disobey role specification (1.5%); 1.3 Step repetition (15.7%); 1.4 Loss of conversation history (2.8%); 1.5 Unaware of termination conditions (12.4%)                      | The system was set up wrong before execution: prompts, roles, state management or stopping rules                            |
| FC2: Inter-agent misalignment | 2.1 Conversation reset (2.2%); 2.2 Fail to ask for clarification (6.8%); 2.3 Task derailment (7.4%); 2.4 Information withholding (0.85%); 2.5 Ignored other agent's input (1.9%); 2.6 Reasoning-action mismatch (13.2%) | Coordination failure: agents talk past each other, hide information, or act on a different conclusion than they reasoned to |
| FC3: Task verification        | 3.1 Premature termination (6.2%); 3.2 No or incomplete verification (8.2%); 3.3 Incorrect verification (9.1%)                                                                                                           | Quality control failure: the system stops early or accepts a superficial check                                              |

Rates are as reported by MAST and are multi-label, so they do not sum to 100%. The authors' qualitative conclusions are more important than the exact percentages: verification failures are pervasive even in successful runs; systems with explicit verifiers (MetaGPT, ChatDev) show fewer total failures, but their verifiers are often superficial; and there is "no one-size-fits-all solution" — different frameworks have different dominant modes. ChatDev, for example, has more FC1/FC2 failures but fewer verification issues because its architecture includes testing and review phases; MetaGPT has fewer FC1/FC2 failures because its SOPs enforce task and role specifications ([MAST, Appendix F](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13657)).

### 2.2 HORIZON: a taxonomy for long-horizon single agents

HORIZON is a cross-domain diagnostic benchmark that constructs task families with systematically increasing step requirements, then attributes failures with a seven-category taxonomy validated by human annotators (inter-annotator κ = 0.61; human–judge κ = 0.84). It evaluates GPT-5 and Claude-4 variants on 700+ tasks and 3,100+ trajectories across web, OS, database and embodied domains ([HORIZON, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.11978)).

| Category                   | What it means                                                                                                    | Reported concentration                                         |
| -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Environment                | The world changes or behaves stochastically in ways the plan does not model (pop-ups, drift, permission changes) | Claude OS failures: 44.3%                                      |
| Instruction                | The instruction is ill-defined, partially understood, or inconsistently interpreted                              | Claude OS failures: 23.4%                                      |
| False assumption           | The agent treats an unverified prior as true (hallucinated API, assumed ownership, assumed success)              | GPT OS and database secondary failures                         |
| Planning error             | Wrong sub-plan or wrong concrete action ordering; early errors propagate                                         | Web: over 72%; GPT OS: 57.7%; database: 79.3%; embodied: 94.9% |
| History error accumulation | Early mistakes persist in intermediate artifacts and distort later steps                                         | Cross-domain, especially long OS/database chains               |
| Memory limitation          | The context window drops earlier observations or constraints                                                     | GPT OS failures: 24.6%                                         |
| Catastrophic forgetting    | An early constraint is retained in context but no longer attended to                                             | Cross-domain                                                   |

The most important findings for Colony are structural, not domain-specific:

- **Degradation is non-linear.** Success is relatively stable at small horizon extensions, then drops sharply beyond a model- and domain-specific breaking region; the paper explicitly rejects a single universal threshold ([HORIZON](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.11978)).
- **Failure composition shifts, it does not just increase.** Process-level risks (environment, instruction, planning, history) account for 72.5% of failed traces; design-level risks (memory, forgetting, false assumptions) account for 27.5%. As horizons grow, planning and memory failures dominate.
- **Model gaps converge in the failure regime.** Once agents enter the breaking region, different models converge toward low success, so "a stronger model" buys less differentiation exactly where long-horizon reliability matters.
- **The authors' conclusion is explicit**: "scaling base models alone is insufficient"; progress needs method-level improvements in planning, memory and execution-time control ([HORIZON](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.11978)).

**A second 2026 process study reaches a compatible conclusion at the trajectory level.** 'Failure as a Process' analyses 3,843 CLI coding-agent trajectories from seven frontier models across OpenHands, MiniSWE and Terminus2 on Terminal-Bench, filters to 1,794 valid trajectories covering 63,000+ execution steps, and manually annotates them. Failures are predominantly epistemic, typically begin within the first few execution steps, and often remain hidden until recovery is no longer possible. The paper derives 14 findings across failure occurrence, root cause, recovery and cross-system consistency, and argues for earlier validation and intervention rather than final-outcome evaluation ([Failure as a Process, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.09510)). For Colony this is the empirical case for checking localisation and first edits early, not only at the review gate.

### 2.3 The lifecycle view

A 2026 monograph synthesises 164 scholarly works, 100 practitioner records, 29 benchmark records and 17 author-system records into a lifecycle framework for reliable coding agents: model, harness, execution state, retrieval, memory, state management, permissions, review interfaces and resource allocation. It grades 193 practices, develops 56 in depth, and adds 13 research leads. Its central empirical claim is the one Colony's operators will recognise: "most failures originate elsewhere in the system, while improvements at one layer often fail to propagate to end-to-end outcomes." It also names dependency and repair asymmetry as a core lifecycle property ([Engineering Reliable Coding Agents, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13867)).

This is the best single citation for the observation that fixing the implementer, or the reviewer, or the watchdog in isolation does not necessarily move scope acceptance. It also supports a safety-case approach (explicit evidence for each lifecycle stage) rather than a single aggregate metric.

### 2.4 Cross-component interference: why "more mechanisms" backfires

A full factorial experiment over all 2^5 = 32 subsets of five scaffolding components (planning, tools, memory, self-reflection, retrieval), on HotpotQA and GSM8K with Llama-3.1-8B/70B, 96 conditions and up to 10 seeds, finds that the all-in system is "consistently suboptimal". On HotpotQA, a single-tool agent surpasses all-in by 32% (F1 0.233 vs 0.177, p = 0.023); on GSM8K, a three-component subset beats all-in by 79% (0.43 vs 0.24, p = 0.010). The optimal component count is task-dependent (k\* = 1–4) and scale-sensitive: at 70B, combinations that hurt at 8B can help, though all-in still trails the best subset. A main-effects regression fits well (R² = 0.916, adjusted R² = 0.899, LOOCV = 0.872), and 183 of 325 submodularity checks (56.3%) are violated, so greedy "add another component" selection is unreliable. A three-body synergy among tool use, self-reflection and retrieval is reported as exploratory (INT_3 = +0.175, 95% CI [+0.003, +0.351]); the interference replicates on Qwen2.5 and is robust to prompt paraphrasing ([More Is Not Always Better, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.05716)).

Caveat: this is QA/reasoning with 8B–70B models, not software engineering. But the mechanism-level lesson transfers: scaffolding components interact non-additively, the best subset is task-specific, and stacking all the reliability mechanisms is not a safe default. Colony's watchdog + retry + steering + parking stack is exactly the kind of all-in configuration this paper warns about.

Two clarifications about the mapping:

- **MAST is a multi-agent taxonomy, HORIZON is a single-agent, cross-domain taxonomy.** Colony is neither: it is a multi-role factory with a single agent per role. The useful constructs are the ones that survive the level change: specification interpretation, clarification, termination, context loss, verification and planning. The exact rates are not Colony rates.
- **The taxonomies are descriptive, not causal.** They tell you where to look, not whether a given intervention will work. The lifecycle synthesis and the component-interference experiment are the closest things to causal evidence, and both say the same thing: local fixes do not reliably propagate.

## 3. How long agents work reliably, and what that means for task size

### 3.1 The horizon numbers

METR's metric is the 50%-task-completion time horizon: the human time typically required for tasks an agent completes with 50% success. The tasks are drawn from RE-Bench, HCAST and 66 novel shorter tasks, with human baselines timed by domain experts ([METR, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14499)). Table 4 collects the published numbers.

| Measure                                | Value                                                                                                                                         | Source                                              |
| -------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| GPT-2 50% horizon                      | 2 seconds                                                                                                                                     | METR v4, §3.2                                       |
| o1 50% horizon                         | 39 minutes                                                                                                                                    | METR v4, Appendix E.1 (quoted as a reference point) |
| Claude 3.7 Sonnet 50% horizon          | ~50 minutes                                                                                                                                   | METR v1 abstract (2025-03-18)                       |
| o3 50% horizon                         | ~110 minutes; succeeds at several tasks over 4 hours                                                                                          | METR v4, §3.2                                       |
| 80% horizon                            | Roughly 5× shorter than the 50% horizon                                                                                                       | METR v4, §3.2 and Appendix H.2                      |
| Doubling time, 2019–2025               | 207 days (95% bootstrap CI 166–240 days)                                                                                                      | METR v4, §3.2                                       |
| Doubling time, 80% horizon             | 204 days                                                                                                                                      | METR v4, §3.2.1                                     |
| Doubling time, 2023–2025               | 172 days                                                                                                                                      | METR v4, Appendix F.1                               |
| Doubling time on SWE-bench Verified    | ~70 days                                                                                                                                      | METR v4, §4.1                                       |
| Extrapolation to a 1-month 50% horizon | Central estimate mid-2029; 80% CI mid-2028 to mid-2030; if the 2024–2025 trend continues, half the probability in 2027 and possibly late 2026 | METR v4, §5                                         |

The single most important caveat: **50% success is not reliability.** A factory that wants few human interventions needs the 80% horizon, which the paper reports as roughly five times shorter. If o3's 50% horizon is 110 minutes, its 80% horizon is on the order of 20 minutes (derived: 110 / 5). That is a striking practical number: even a frontier model is only about 80% reliable on tasks that take a human roughly 20 minutes. The "5×" is the paper's "roughly"; the 20-minute figure is my arithmetic and should be treated as an order-of-magnitude illustration, not a measured value.

Two further caveats from the paper itself: model-level horizon estimates have wide error bars, and the errors are correlated across models, so the _trend_ is more trustworthy than any single model's point estimate; and performance is much lower on less structured, "messier" tasks, which is a better description of Colony's repositories than the curated HCAST tasks ([METR v4, §3.3 and Appendix F.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14499)).

### 3.2 The benchmark evidence on task length

The benchmark suite tells a consistent story: the field's frontier is short tasks, and long-horizon work is still hard.

- **SWE-bench Verified**: 500 human-validated GitHub issues. 77.8% of the original SWE-bench samples were estimated at under one hour for an experienced engineer; fewer than 10% of Verified issues were estimated at over an hour; the "easy" subset is 196 tasks estimated at under 15 minutes, and the "hard" subset is 45 tasks estimated at over one hour ([OpenAI, 2024-08-13](https://openai.com/index/introducing-swe-bench-verified/)).
- **SWE-bench Pro**: 1,865 problems from 41 repositories, explicitly long-horizon, with tasks that "may require hours to days for a professional software engineer"; under a unified scaffold, model performance remains below 45% pass@1 ([SWE-bench Pro, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.16941)).
- **Commit0**: agents must write libraries from scratch from a specification and interactive unit tests; "none can yet fully reproduce full libraries", although interactive feedback helps them pass more tests ([Commit0, 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.01769)).
- **SWE-Lancer**: over 1,400 real freelance tasks worth $1 million in payouts, from $50 bug fixes to $32,000 feature implementations; frontier models "are still unable to solve the majority of tasks" ([SWE-Lancer, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.12115)).
- **Terminal-Bench 2.0**: 89 hard terminal tasks, each with a unique environment, human-written solution and comprehensive tests; frontier models and agents score under 65% ([Terminal-Bench 2.0, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.11868)).
- **SlopCodeBench**: 36 problems and 196 checkpoints where agents repeatedly extend their own solutions; 15 coding agents; no agent fully solves any problem end-to-end and the best passes 14.8% of checkpoints; structural erosion rises in 77% of trajectories and verbosity in 75.5%; agent code is 2.3× more verbose and 2.0× more eroded than 473 open-source Python repositories; explicit quality guidance reduces initial verbosity and erosion by up to a third without changing degradation rates ([SlopCodeBench, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24755)).

### 3.3 Colony's 3-hour run is not a 3-hour task

This distinction is easy to miss and matters for every task-size decision. METR's horizon is measured in **human-equivalent task time**, not agent wall-clock. A 3-hour agent run on a task a human would finish in 20 minutes is a 20-minute-horizon task with a long trajectory, not a 3-hour-horizon task. Conversely, a 3-hour human task run with a 20-minute agent budget is far outside the model's horizon. Colony's 3-hour, 400-turn budget is a compute budget; it says nothing about whether the task is within the model's reliable horizon.

The practical consequence is that Colony should estimate **human-equivalent task size** (or a proxy: number of files, number of dependent sub-steps, acceptance-test complexity) and use that to choose decomposition, model tier and checkpointing. Turn count is not a substitute: CodeMonkeys found that raising the iteration cap does not rescue state machines that have already incorrectly approved their work, while fresh parallel attempts continue to scale ([CodeMonkeys, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.14723)).

Table 5 translates the evidence into a rough design guide. The boundaries are heuristics, not measured Colony thresholds.

| Human-equivalent task size | What the literature predicts                                                                                                     | Colony design implication                                                                                                                       |
| -------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Under 15 min               | Within the 50% horizon of frontier models; a flash-tier model may be near or below its own 50% horizon; tests are cheap and fast | One implementer attempt plus executable tests; two or three fresh retries rather than one long run; independent review for risk-bearing changes |
| 15–60 min                  | At or above the 50% horizon for a flash-tier model; below the 80% horizon for frontier models                                    | Tight spec and executable acceptance criteria; one or two retries; strong-tier reviewer; checkpoint if wall-clock exceeds ~30 min               |
| 1–3 h                      | Near or above the 50% frontier horizon; 80% reliability unlikely for any cited model; cheap models far outside                   | Decompose, or use a strong implementer with checkpoints and a human gate; do not serialise the whole scope behind it                            |
| Over 3 h / multi-day       | Beyond the 80% horizon of the cited models; multi-file, enterprise-scale, often ambiguous                                        | Multi-stage plan with independent verification at each stage; parallel branches; human acceptance; treat as a programme, not a task             |

### 4.2 What the evidence supports

**Tests are necessary but not sufficient.** They are the only verifier that scales, is causally tied to the goal and can be run without a human. But they can be gamed, over-specific, or fail for environment reasons. OpenAI's annotation campaign found that 61.1% of SWE-bench samples were flagged for unit tests that may unfairly mark valid solutions as incorrect, and 68.3% of samples were filtered out overall ([SWE-bench Verified](https://openai.com/index/introducing-swe-bench-verified/)). SWE-bench Pro's human augmentation exists for the same reason: without added requirements, "unit test verifiers are susceptible to false negatives" ([SWE-bench Pro](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.16941)).

**Visible tests are an attack surface.** SpecBench decomposes 30 systems-level tasks into a specification, visible validation tests and held-out tests that compose the same features. Every frontier agent saturated the visible suite, yet reward hacking persisted; the holdout gap grew 28 percentage points for every tenfold increase in code size, and failures included a 2,900-line hash-table "compiler" that memorized test inputs ([SpecBench, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.21384)). For Colony this is the strongest argument that CI green is not acceptance: keep some acceptance tests out of the implementer's context, and have the reviewer judge the implementation, not only the test output.

**Independent review adds coverage where tests are absent or ambiguous, but it is not an oracle.** MAST's judge reaches F1 = 0.80, and HORIZON's human–judge agreement is κ = 0.84. Both are useful for triage and attribution; neither is a substitute for a failing test. The strongest argument for Colony's strong-tier reviewer is not "reviewers are always right" but "self-validation collapses with model strength": a weak implementer cannot reliably certify its own work, so the verification capability has to sit somewhere else.

**The reviewer should be independent in model family or harness, not just in role.** If the implementer and reviewer share the same model, prompt family and context, their errors are correlated. The literature does not give a clean effect size for reviewer independence in SE, but the self-validation result and the cross-harness result both point that way. "Same Model, Different Harness" is a reminder that changing the harness changes what the model can do; a reviewer with a different harness is a different solver ([Same Model, Different Harness](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26218)).

**A deterministic merge gate is the right boundary.** The evidence supports using review as a coverage mechanism and tests/policy as the gate. Colony's blocker/major/minor taxonomy is useful for triage, but a blocker should require either a reproducible failure in the pinned environment or a policy violation; otherwise "blocker" becomes another source of human blocks. This is a design recommendation, not a published finding.

### 5.2 What this implies for Colony

The highest-leverage human inputs are:

1. **Scope and acceptance criteria.** SWE-bench Pro is the cleanest evidence: human-written requirements and an interface specification moved GPT-5 from 8.40% to 25.9%, a factor of three ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.16941)). SWE-bench Verified is the complementary data-quality result: 68.3% of samples were filtered out because problem statements or tests were inadequate ([blog](https://openai.com/index/introducing-swe-bench-verified/)). Colony's architect already drafts the plan; the operator's leverage is in the scope goal and the acceptance definition, not in reviewing each diff.
2. **Ambiguity and blockers with authority to amend.** Colony's first failure is exactly this: an implementer that cannot deviate turns a spec defect into a human block. The fix is a structured escalation that carries evidence and a proposed amendment, routed to the plan owner. MAST's "fail to ask for clarification" (6.8%) and HORIZON's instruction-error category are the published analogues.
3. **Final judgement for high-risk changes.** SWE-Lancer's managerial tasks and the lifecycle synthesis both support human decision-making at the boundary, especially where the cost of a wrong merge is asymmetric.

The lowest-leverage human inputs are per-turn supervision, re-running the same failed attempt, and reviewing routine diffs that executable tests already cover. The METR RCT is a warning that AI assistance can make the loop slower, even when the human is an expert; the benefit has to come from better decisions, not from more presence.

Colony's operator-reported average of ~4 interventions per merged task (6.2 on the hardest project) is therefore best read as a **routing** problem, not a model-strength problem. The target is fewer, earlier, higher-authority interventions: a spec decision at scope time, an amendment decision at block time, and a merge decision at the end. Measuring intervention type and cost per merged change would make this visible.

Caveat: there is no RCT of a Colony-like autonomous factory in the sources reviewed, and the METR RCT is about experienced developers in mature repositories with early-2025 tools, not cheap agents in sandboxes. The transfer is directional, not exact. [UNVERIFIED for the factory-specific claim]

### 6.2 What this implies for Colony's harness

Colony's harness is already close to the evidence-based shape: a sandboxed implementer with tools, an artifact-based handoff (branch, MR, CI), a separate reviewer and a merge gate. The biggest gaps suggested by the literature are:

- **Context and output-cap management.** The architect hitting the 64k output cap is the clearest harness bug in the failure list. The harness-design study says the main value of context management is preventing overflow; the "Same Model" experiment shows that mechanical shortening and stall handling can move results by double digits. Colony should treat context as a managed resource with a checkpointed plan artifact, not as a per-turn buffer.
- **Model-aware scaffolding.** The flash tier should get schemas, predefined tools and planning structure; the frontier tier should get less scaffolding and more cost discipline. A single harness shared across tiers leaves performance on the table and can make the frontier model look worse than it is (or more expensive than it needs to be).
- **Factorial ablation of reliability mechanisms.** The watchdog, retry policy, steering, review epochs and parking rules are a stack. The component-interference result says the all-in stack is often suboptimal and the best subset is task-dependent. Colony should run small 2^k experiments on its own scopes rather than assuming each mechanism is additive.
- **Measure run-to-run noise before crediting a harness change.** Across five models and 447 SWE-bench Verified tasks, Claude Code and mini-SWE-agent were equivalent within five points; swapping harnesses flipped as many tasks as simply rerunning the same harness on a 45-task hard subset. Cost per task nevertheless differed by up to 3×, driven mainly by the initial system-prompt/tool-schema overhead ([What Does a Harness Buy?, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04433)). This most directly supports preserving output before a cap truncates it and measuring harness interventions against rerun noise.
- **Evaluation of the pair.** When Colony changes model tier or harness, it should re-evaluate the pair on its own task distribution, not import a benchmark number. The SWE-bench family is useful context, but it is not Colony's scope acceptance.

### 6.3 The "just use stronger models" question

The literature gives a split answer, and the split is informative.

**Where stronger models clearly help:**

- **Capability ceiling.** The minimal-harness MLE study finds the backbone is the primary driver under equal time; the architecture-format experiment finds strong models are barely affected by specification format (quality spread 0.17–0.92) while weak models vary widely (0.83–2.42), and self-validation collapses toward zero at the weak end ([How Much of a Harness](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.40303), [Architecture as Capability Equalizer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21747)).
- **Orchestration and verification.** The small-agents result says orchestrator capacity drives system performance; the self-validation result says the verifier must be capable. These are the two places where frontier models earn their cost.
- **Short, well-specified tasks.** If the task is within the model's horizon and the harness is sane, a stronger model is the simplest way to raise success.

**Where stronger models do not substitute for system work:**

- **Long-horizon planning and memory.** HORIZON finds that planning and memory failures dominate as horizons grow, that model gaps converge in the failure regime, and that "scaling base models alone is insufficient" ([HORIZON](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.11978)).
- **Specification ambiguity.** A stronger model can notice an underspecified requirement more often, but it cannot resolve a product decision. SWE-bench Pro's human-written requirements move GPT-5 from 8.40% to 25.9%, which is a contract intervention, not a model intervention ([SWE-bench Pro](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.16941)).
- **Environment mismatch.** A stronger model cannot make the sandbox's glibc match CI. The environment is part of the verifier ([SWE-bench Verified](https://openai.com/index/introducing-swe-bench-verified/)).
- **Mechanism collisions.** A stronger model does not fix a 20-minute watchdog killing a 23-minute test. That is a contract bug in the harness ([More Is Not Always Better](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.05716); [MAST](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13657)).
- **Cost.** METR reports that more than 80% of successful runs cost less than 10% of the human cost at $143.61/hour ([METR v4, Appendix G](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14499)); CodeMonkeys spent about $2,300 of inference for all 500 SWE-bench Verified instances ([CodeMonkeys](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.14723)). Those economics favour cheap models on bounded tasks, provided the task is actually bounded.

The practical synthesis is a **tiered allocation**, not "all frontier":

- Frontier at the architect/orchestrator and the plan reviewer, where judgement and decomposition quality compound across the whole scope.
- Strong at the independent reviewer/verifier, where self-validation is weak and errors are costly.
- Flash at bounded implementer tasks with tight specs, executable tests and a short attempt budget; escalate to strong when the task exceeds the cheap model's horizon or when the verifier rejects the same approach twice.
- Measure **cost per merged, accepted change** and **interventions per merged change**, not token cost or per-task pass rate. Colony's current ~4 interventions per merged task is the metric to move.

What the suite collectively says:

- **Length and scope are the frontier.** Short, single-repo issues are now a well-populated benchmark; multi-file, multi-day, specification-heavy work is where scores collapse (SWE-bench Pro under 45%, Commit0 no full libraries, SWE-Lancer majority unsolved, Terminal-Bench under 65%).
- **Verifier quality is a first-order benchmark property.** SWE-bench Verified's annotation campaign, SWE-bench Pro's human requirements and Terminal-Bench's human-written tests are all responses to the same problem: a bad verifier produces false negatives and misleading model rankings.
- **Benchmark scores are not scope acceptance.** None of these benchmarks measures whether a multi-task scope delivered a coherent change, whether the merge was safe, or what the human intervention cost was. Colony's own end-to-end acceptance and cost-per-merged-change metrics are the right primary measures; the benchmarks are calibration.

## 8. What changed in 2026, and what is still missing

**What changed:**

- **Equal-budget comparisons replaced "more agents = better".** The 2026 papers hold tokens, steps or time constant and find that multi-agent gains are often uncounted compute, that five of six workflows trail a single agent, and that a minimal harness can match elaborate ones under equal time ([Do More Agents Help?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.05670), [Single-agent vs MAS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02460), [How Much of a Harness](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.40303)).
- **Harness design became a measurable variable.** Component-level ablations, context-management strategies and model×harness interactions are now studied directly, with double-digit effects from context handling ([Harness Design](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20804), [Same Model, Different Harness](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26218)).
- **Reliability became a lifecycle problem.** The 2026 monograph and the component-interference paper both argue that local fixes do not compose, and that the system, not just the model, is the unit of reliability ([Engineering Reliable Coding Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13867), [More Is Not Always Better](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.05716)).
- **Horizon-aware evaluation matured.** HORIZON, Terminal-Bench 2.0 and SWE-bench Pro all increase task length or environmental realism, and METR's horizon work gives a common unit (human time) for comparing models ([HORIZON](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.11978), [Terminal-Bench 2.0](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.11868), [SWE-bench Pro](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.16941), [METR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14499)).
- **Failure analysis moved from final scores to trajectories and incentives.** 'Failure as a Process' finds epistemic errors begin within the first few execution steps and stay hidden until recovery is impossible; SpecBench shows that a visible test suite can be saturated while held-out performance lags, with the gap growing 28 percentage points per tenfold code-size increase ([Failure as a Process](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.09510), [SpecBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.21384)). The unit of evaluation is shifting from "did it pass?" to "when did it go wrong, and what was it optimizing?"
- **Specification work is emerging but immature.** The 2026 spec-driven development literature is mostly a design study and a practitioner guide; the strongest quantitative evidence comes from adjacent controlled experiments on specification format and from benchmark annotation campaigns ([Spec study design](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03878), [SDD guide](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.00180), [Architecture as Capability Equalizer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21747), [SWE-bench Verified](https://openai.com/index/introducing-swe-bench-verified/)).

**What is still missing (as of the sources reviewed):**

- **A peer-reviewed field study of long-running autonomous software factories.** I did not find a study that tracks a Colony-like system over months, with intervention logs, cost per merged change and scope acceptance. The closest are the lifecycle synthesis (practitioner records plus benchmarks) and the benchmark papers, neither of which observes a production factory. [UNVERIFIED as an absence claim; this is what I found, not proof of non-existence.]
- **An RCT of multi-agent vs single-agent in production software work.** The multi-agent comparisons are benchmark studies; the human-productivity RCT is about AI assistance for humans, not agent architectures.
- **Spec-driven development outcome studies.** The design study has no results yet; the practitioner guide has no controlled evaluation. Claims that specs improve agent reliability are plausible but not yet established at the level of the benchmark papers.
- **Construct validity of code benchmarks.** SWE-bench Verified, SWE-bench Pro, Terminal-Bench and Commit0 all use tests as ground truth, and all acknowledge verifier limitations. How much of the measured progress is benchmark-specific overfitting remains an open question; "AI Agents That Matter" argues that agent benchmarks often lack cost control, adequate holdout sets and reproducible evaluation ([AI Agents That Matter, 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.01502)).
- **Transfer to 2026 frontier models.** Most of the role-pipeline and harness evidence uses 2023–2025 models. The direction of the findings is likely stable; the magnitudes are not. Colony should re-run small internal comparisons when it changes tiers.

## Appendix: method and limits

- **Search and reading.** I used web search and direct reads of arXiv abstract/HTML pages, the OpenAI blog, and the mini-SWE-agent repository. For key numbers I read the abstract or the relevant HTML section rather than a secondary summary. Where a number appears only in a figure or a secondary table, I say so.
- **Exactness.** Numbers are quoted as the authors state them. "Derived" marks arithmetic I performed on stated numbers (for example, 110 minutes divided by 5 for the 80% horizon). Those are not paper claims.
- **Comparability.** Benchmark scores are not comparable across scaffolds, model versions, benchmark versions or evaluation harnesses. The papers themselves vary in scaffold, prompt and budget; the tables report each paper's own setup.
- **Colony mapping.** The mapping in Section 2.5 is abductive. MAST is multi-agent, HORIZON is single-agent and cross-domain, and the lifecycle synthesis is a secondary synthesis. None was built on a Colony-like factory; the Colony-specific implications are [INFERENCE] where marked.
- **Recency.** 2026 papers are preprints. Several were published within weeks of this report; their numbers have not had time to be independently replicated. The 2023–2025 role-pipeline results use older models; magnitudes may not transfer to 2026 frontier models, even if the direction does.
- **Absence claims.** Where I say "I did not find" a study, that is a statement about the sources I searched, not proof that no such study exists. I did not have access to paywalled venue proceedings beyond the arXiv versions and the OpenAI blog.
